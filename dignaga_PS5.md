# Diṅnāga on the Incommensurability of Perception and Language

A translation and discussion of the Pramāṇasamuccayaḥ, chapter V, verses 1-4 and svavr̥ttiḥ. 

## Discussion

In the fifth chapter of his magnum opus, *On Measurement* (Pramāṇasamuccayaḥ), Diṅnāga develops one of his most profound and disturbing insights into the nature of human cognition: the incommensurability of perception with language.

The incommensurability thesis (pramāṇavyavasthāvādaḥ) states that there can be no transfer of information between perception and language. In the direction of perception to language, this means that we can never justify any claim with reference to experience nor claim to know the contents of any experiential state. This creates a symmetry between the first and third person perspective. It is almost a cliche to say to another, “You could never know how I feel!”. But if Diṅnāga is right then *I* don’t know how I feel either. It also leaves the status of empirical knowledge in an uncertain place. What does it mean to say that I know facts about the natural world if the information captured by that knowledge does not pass from nature to mind through sensation? In the direction of language to perception, this means we can never communicate the content of our experience nor capture the structure of experience in language. Insofar as all science—all theory—is fundamentally linguistic, this means there can never be a *science* of consciousness. No theory of experience. 

The incommensurability thesis separates our idea of knowledge into two distinct conceptions. On the one hand, we think of knowledge under a metaphor of *illumination*. For something to be known is for it to be present to us, to make a difference to *us* in our cognitive lives. The concept of luminosity is theorized as a distinctively epistemic concept in the first chapter of Diṅnāga’s work. A second conception of knowledge is as something that guides action and brings it under a rational description. We reason from diagnosis to intervention in a step-by-step process which is transparent, public, and capable of evaluation. 

The first kind of knowledge is what Diṅnāga calls “perception”. The second is what he calls “inference”. These two ideas represent two fundamentally different senses of the word “to know” and are only brought together by the illusion of common content. It is on the basis of this illusion that we give a single word to these two radically different phenomena.

The word Diṅnāga gives, which he inherits from a much older tradition, is “measure” (pramāṇam). A measure is something that, in some vague pretheoretic sense of the word, captures information about some aspect of the world: the measurable object (prameyaḥ). The result of the operation of a measure on an object is *measurement* (pramitiḥ). Traditions and philosophers prior to Diṅnāga debated the number, nature, and interrelations of the measures extensively. But the idea that the concept is split into two incommensurable domains is a hallmark of Diṅnāgian thought.

The key to understanding the incommensurability thesis lies in the nature of language. The incommensurability argument appears in the beginning of the fifth chapter of *On Measurement*, in which Diṅnāga works out his general theory of language. The Sanskrit original is lost, but it is preserved in two Tibetan translations and as excerpts quoted in Jinendrabuddhi’s Sanskrit commentary on the text. Quotations from it also survive scattered across sources throughout Sanskrit philosophical literature. The Sanskrit text reproduced in this essay is reconstructed from the extant sources by Ole Pind (2015). The translation, though, is my own.

According to this theory, the linguistic measure—the use of language to capture information about the world—is not distinct from the inferential measure. From the perspective of the theory of measures, language is just inference (na pramāṇāntaram śābdam anumānāt [1]). On this account, linguistic expressions and their mental analogues in thought convey information not by representing the properties of objects but by placing constraints on further language use.

We might compare this to an information-theoretic account of information content where the realization of some random variable conveys information about a system by constraining the possible micro-states that the system could exist in. In the same way, the acquisition of linguistic information about some object constrains the further possibilities of what could be said about that object. 

Linguistic information on the final analysis ends up as nothing more than a symbol’s form (sounds, shapes, or mental appearances) equipped with what Diṅnāga calls “exclusion” (apoha) relations to other information, encoding how expressions block each other. Meaning, in this view, is not a relation between language and the world but a relation internal to language itself. And these relations end up just being relations of entailment and negation. So, semantics is nothing other than logic. This comes to be known as the theory of exclusion (apohavādaḥ).

Before detailing this account, however, Diṅnāga offers an argument for the incommensurability thesis as a motivation for developing an inferential theory of meaning. In what remains of this introduction (prior to the translation itself), I want to give a brief overview and reconstruction of this argument.

Diṅnāga begins by restating the claim just discussed, that there are only two fundamental measures: perception and inference. Prior authors including other Buddhists (such as Vasubandhu) and non-buddhist philosophers such as Vātsyāyana (the author of the Nyāyasūtrabhāṣya) generally accepted linguistic expressions as constituting a third distinct kind of measure. On these older views, a perceptual experience captured some information about an object by rendering its properties in sensory terms. On one common-sense view entertained by Dharmakīrti (a major post-Diṅnāgian figure), sensory features represented properties of objects through a pattern of correlation such that the same properties would trigger the same sensory features across different perceptions. A linguistic expression could capture the same information by representing the object’s properties with symbols such that the presence of the same symbol across different episodes of language use would correspond to the presence of the same properties across different speech contexts.

Under such a framework view, symbols are “connected” (sambandhaḥ) to objects such that a given symbol may only apply to an object to which it is connected. This connection must satisfy two constraints:

1. The connection is formed on the basis of some general *use condition* (pravr̥ttinimittam) that must be satisfied by all and only connected objects.
2. Speakers of the language must learn this condition in order to use the symbol.

What is communicated by the use of a symbol is just that its object satisfies its use condition. 

But Diṅnāga argues that no facts about objects can serve as the use condition for the symbols applied to them. And, in particular, no *perceptual feature* can ever act as the use condition for a symbol purporting to capture perceptual information.

The argument turns on the relationship between a family of symbols called *class terms* (jātiśabdāḥ), which are to be distinguished primarily from proper names by being subject to *subdivision* (bhedārthair apṛthakśruteḥ [2cd]). The key idea is that class terms can represent the same fact at different levels of granularity, such as calling a shade of color “blue” vs “teal”. In this case, the two terms can be used in coordination with one another such that they are in some sense conditioned by the same thing in a given speech context but are not synonymous across contexts. The sense in which they are co-conditioned is itself something to be theorized. But, the pre-theoretical data in need of theorizing are sentences such as:

1. This color is blue.
2. This vehicle is a car.
3. This entity is a substance.

etc.

The point is most clearly seen (no pun intended) in the first case. The words “color” and “blue” co-occur in a sentence in virtue of one and the same perceptual feature. It is because that feature is a color that the word “color” can be used in the sentence describing it. And it is because it is blue that the word “blue” can occur in the same sentence.

The question for us then is this. What is the relationship between the conditions for the words “color” and “blue” such that:

1. The words are not synonymous
2. The same feature satisfies the condition for both words

Whatever theory of class terms we hold must satisfy the two constraints on term coordination along with the earlier two constraints on semantic connections. These four constraints must hold for *any* theory of class terms—and therefore any theory about how language captures general information about facts.

Now consider the following account for the condition of a class term. Suppose that any of the various subdivisions of a class term like ‘blue’—e.g. ‘teal’, ‘turquoise’, ‘navy’, etc.—can act as a separate condition for the parent term. So, something is ‘blue’ just in the case that it is ‘teal’ *or* ‘turquoise’ *or* ‘navy’, etc. Diṅnāga refers to this as a class term directly signifying its subdivisions (jātiśabdo bhedānāṃ [2a]).

Diṅnāga begins the reduction by showing that a class term cannot directly signify its subdivisions. The reason is that the use condition for a class term does not, in principle, fix the number of subdivisions it can have. Nothing about the meaning of the word ‘blue’ precludes the possibility of encountering a shade of blue you have never seen before—no matter how many blue shades you have encountered thus far. This means that the condition for a class term that directly signifies its subdivisions would have an infinite number of elements. But such a condition can never be learned. And this would violate the second constraint on semantic connections. Diṅnāga refers to this as the problem of infinity (ānantyāt. [2b1]).

On the other hand, if we allow a class term to have different use conditions across different applications, then we would not need to learn the full disjunction to apply it in coordination with any particular subdivision. But this would violate the first constraint on semantic connections. Since one class term could operate under multiple use conditions, we would not know which condition is operational in a particular use and would remain in doubt about what information the term is meant to convey. Diṅnāga calls this the problem of inconstancy (vyabhicārataḥ. [2b2]).

Another possibility is that the condition for a class term involves a completely different object feature than the terms for its subdivision. That is to say, ‘blue’ and ‘teal’ simply refer to different perceptual features. But then, obviously, they would violate the second constraint on term coordination. Or they refer to the same feature and end up synonymous, violating the first constraint on term coordination. Diṅnāga refers to this as the problem of coordination (bhedārthair apṛthakśruteḥ. [2cd]). 

There is, however, another way to understand this possibility. Namely, that each class term involves a different condition but one and the same object feature satisfies both conditions. Diṅnāga refers to this idea as a class term signifying an object as belonging to a class (tadvato [4a]). In this case, the shared object between two class terms becomes the basis for their coordination. And the differing classes form the basis for their difference in meaning. Thus the problem of coordination is easily answered.

In response to this, Diṅnāga provides a deeper reason for why class terms in a class-subdivision relation cannot have different conditions. This is because their semantic content would be logically independent—the class term condition would not imply the possibility of subdivision and the subdivision term condition would not entail the class term condition. So, their connection to the same features would be an empirical fact about the respective terms, but not a logical fact.

But, this is not how the relation between class and subdivision works. There is no such thing as a generic blue shade. We can only conceive of particular shades, never a generic ‘blue’. Similarly, when we learn that a shade of color is ‘teal’, we automatically understand that it is ‘blue’, assuming we also know the term ‘blue’. The conditions of these terms are logically connected—semantically connected—such that one cannot be fully separated from the other and each treated as independent conditions that merely *happen* to be satisfied by the same features. Diṅnāga calls this the problem of non-independence (nāsvatantratvāt [4a]).

Diṅnāga’s discussion of the coordination problem ends here. However, there is another possibility worth considering. Namely, what if the use condition of a class term is complex—involving the conjunction of multiple primitive conditions. Internal relations between class and subdivision terms can be explained in terms of a shared kernel, with different subdivisions differing from each other via additional conditions in the conjunction. In such a case, the model of use conditions as distinct classes would break down since the concept of classes models only logically simple use conditions.

Diṅnāga does not discuss this issue explicitly, perhaps because he takes it for granted that all complex terms can be rewritten via their definitions into primitive class terms (involving class based use conditions) that are still subdivisible and coordinable. This assumption is not obviously true, though it does seem like it would be acceptable to his main interlocutors given their metaphysical commitments; e.g., the account of universals defended by the Naiyyāyikas. However, even if we do not accept this assumption for class terms in general, something more can be said in this regard where specifically perceptual features are concerned.

The key idea is to borrow a proposal from Dharmakīrti, called the co-experience constraint (*sahopalambhaniyamaḥ*). The co-experience constraint is meant to be a criterion of identity for perceptual features. Dharmakīrti uses this in a different context but we can apply it here. The criterion is simple. It states that

(Co-experience Constraint) If it is not possible to isolate two perceptual features from each other in any experience, then they are one and the same feature.
{: .quote}

In other words, what it *means* for a perceptual experience to have distinct features is for elements of that experience to be separable in our attention. Otherwise, we have no criterion of identification for them as distinct features in the first place. What is it that makes, e.g., smell and taste distinct features? Or color and texture? etc. It is that we can isolate them, attend to them, focus on them separately. Otherwise, nothing distinguishes one feature from another. Whereas for “blue” and “teal”, there is nothing about the visual feature that can be isolated in the experience of that color and connected with one term or another. Notions like hue, contrast, and shade are also only separable as abstractions but not isolatable as distinct feature in an experience. This is another way of saying that individual sensory features are *simple*.

So, if we accept the co-experience constraint, the connection of a class term to a single perceptual feature cannot involve the satisfaction of a complex condition. For in that case each primitive condition in the complex would either involve reference to distinct perceptual features or would involve elements that were not present in the perceptual experience. And if the satisfaction of a use condition involves factors not accessible to perception then we would have no way of knowing if a given perceptual feature satisfies the use condition of a given term. Therefore the use conditions connecting class terms to perceptual features must be simple—hence, subject to Diṅnāga’s class-membership model. But, Diṅnāga has already shown that class terms cannot be coherently assigned use conditions under a class-membership model. So, perceptual features cannot satisfy the use conditions for class terms and information about such features cannot be conveyed by the use of such terms.

There is one other possibility to consider in this respect—namely making a metaphysical move in response to Dharmakīrti’s phenomenological (experiential) criterion of identification for perceptual features. This approach is already suggested by earlier Vaiśeṣika authors like Praśastapāda, who argue for the existence of so-called epistemic inseparability (ayutasiddhiḥ) relations between certain kinds of objects. The idea, especially as detailed by the late-classical Naiyyāyika, Jayanta Bhaṭṭa, is that certain objects—prototypically sensory qualities and the substances they inhere in—enjoy an especially close sort of relationship such that they do not register as distinct items in any measurement (any sensory or inferential grasping) where they occur together. They are only separable by observing multiple instances and abstracting from them. So, the redness of the apple is not separable from the substance of the apple in any single instance. But, by comparing apples of different colors, we can separate the colors out conceptually through contrast.

The philosopher Kumārila Bhaṭṭa (normally a critic of Diṅnāga’s project) levels a famous attack against such a relation of  epistemic inseparability. The argument starts with the observation that all relations are in some sense posterior to their relata. That is to say, they depend on their relata for their existence and informational content. Relations are themselves empty of content—and so unmeasurable—when abstracted from all relata. However, if relata are in a relation of epistemic inseparability then they are informationally incomplete without their relation to each other. This means that the relation depends for its content on the relata—and the relata depend for their content on the relation. This is circular, which Kumārila takes to be decisive against the account. There are unresolved issues here regarding what exactly constitutes the informational incompleteness of the relata and to what extent this poses a genuine metaphysical problem rather than an epistemic one. But, we will rest the case here and introduce the text itself.

## Text and Translation

### The Inferential Theory of Meaning

#### Sanskrit

uktaṃ pramāṇadvayam. kecic chābdam api [pramāṇāntaraṃ manyante].

na pramāṇāntaraṃ śābdam anumānāt. tathā hi saḥ \\\\ kṛtakatvādivat svārtham anyāpohena bhāṣate [1]
{: .quote}

śabdo hi yatra viṣaye prayujyate tasya yenāṃśenāvinābhāvitvasambandhaḥ, taṃ kṛtakatvādivad arthāntaravyavacchedena dyotayati, [tasmād anumānān] na bhidyate.

#### English

We have claimed that there are just two measures. But some think that the linguistic measure is distinct:

The linguistic measure is not distinct from inference. Just as with a reason such as “because it is produced”, an expression presents its own information by excluding other information.
{: .quote}

When a linguistic expression is applied to an object it indicates that aspect of the object with which it is invariably connected by excluding other information in the same way as a reason in an inference, as in “[it is impermanent] because it is produced”. So the linguistic measure is not distinct from inference.

### Infinity and Inconstancy

#### Sanskrit

ye tv āhuḥ: jātiśabdaḥ svabhedān sarvān evāha, ukteṣu tu niyamārthaṃ viśeṣaśrutir [iti]. [tatrocyate]

na jātiśabdo bhedānāṃ [2a]
{: .quote}

“vācaka” iti vakṣyate. jātiśabdas tāvat sadādir [dravyādīnāṃ na vācakaḥ],

ānantyāt. [2b1]
{: .quote}

ānantye hi bhedānām aśakyaḥ sambandhaḥ kartum. na cākṛtasambandhe śabde ʾrthābhidhānaṃ yuktaṃ svarūpamātrapratīteḥ.

kiṃ ca,

vyabhicārataḥ. [2b2]
{: .quote}

yathā hi sacchabdo dravye vartate tathā guṇādiṣv apīti vyabhicārāt saṃśayaḥ syāt, nābhidhānam.

#### English

To those who say a class term signifies all of its subdivisions and a specific term serves only to select among them: we reply,

A class term is not of its subdivisions
{: .quote}

The word “signifier” must be supplied. A class term like ‘entity’ does not signify its subdivisions like substance etc. 

because of infinity.
{: .quote}

Subdivisions are infinite so no connection could be established. Without establishing a connection, an expression cannot convey any information since it would generate awareness only of its own form.

Moreover,

Due to inconstancy.
{: .quote}

The term ‘entity’ is applied to qualities, etc., just as it is to substances. Therefore its use is not constant and there would be doubt about its meaning. No signification would occur.

### Coordination

#### Sanskrit

yo ʾpi manyate: [jātiśabdo jātimātre tadyogamātre vā] sambandhasaukaryād avyabhicārāc ceti. tad ayuktam, [na hi tayor api]

vācako yogajātyor vā bhedārthair apṛthakśruteḥ. [2cd]
{: .quote}

tathā hi [sad dravyam, san guṇaḥ, sat karmeti bhedārthair dravyādiśabdaiḥ] sāmānādhikaraṇyaṃ na syāt. tac ca dṛṣṭam. na hi sattā [tadyogo vā] dravyaṃ guṇo vā bhavati, kiṃ tarhi, dravyasya guṇasya vā. āha ca:

vibhaktibhedo niyamād guṇaguṇyabhidhāyinoḥ \\\\ sāmānādhikaraṇyasya prasiddhir dravyaśabdayoḥ. [VP III 14.8]
{: .quote}

sambandhaś cātra sambandhidharmeṇa vācya ucyate: \\\\ tathā hi bhāvaḥ kṛtvoktaḥ, bhāvaś cānyena yujyate. [3]
{: .quote}

sambandhanaṃ hi sambandhaḥ. [so ʾnyena yujyate rāgādivat]. tasmāt sambandhidharmeṇa sambandho vācya iti kṛtvāśaṅkitaṃ svadharmeṇa tu nāsti sambandhasya vācakaḥ śabda ity idaṃ tat prati nāsti. ato naivasya jātiśabdena vācyatvam upapadyate.

#### English

Now, to those who think that a class term just signifies a class or a class’s relation to its instances since establishing a connection would then be feasible and its use would be constant: this does not work because even in this case

It does not signify a relation or a class because then there would be no coordination with terms for subdivisions.
{: .quote}

In this case, there would be no coordination of class terms with terms for subdivisions as in “a substance entity”, “a quality entity”, “an action entity”, etc. But this is observed. Moreover, neither the existence class nor its relation to its instances *is* a substance or quality. Rather, it is *of* the substance or quality. For, as [Bhartr̥hari] notes:

A property and its substrate are signified with a difference in case-ending. Coordination is only between two substrate level terms.
{: .quote}
(Vākyapadīyam III.14.8)
{: .examplecaption}

Moreover, a relation is signified with reference to a property of the relatum. In such a case, it is first treated as an action and then signified. But an action still requires a separate relation to connect it to its arguments.
{: .quote}

A relation is construed as an act of relating and connected to its relatum by a separate relation just like a quality (e.g. color). Given that a relation can only be signified with reference to a property of the relatum, the worry that an expression cannot signify a relation with respect to the relation’s own property does not arise. [But then what is signified is not the relation as such.] Therefore, a class term cannot be said to signify a relation.

### Non-independence

#### Sanskrit

[ye tv āhuḥ]\: viśeṣaśabdaiḥ [sāmānādhikaraṇyāt sambandhasaukāryād avyabhicārāc ca jātimanmātraṃ vivakṣitam iti. tatra]

tadvato nāsvatantratvāt. [4a]
{: .quote}

evam api hi sacchabdo jātisvarūpamātropasarjanaṃ dravyam āha, na sākṣād iti tadgataghaṭādibhedānākṣepād atadbhedatve sāmānādhikaraṇyābhāvaḥ. na hy asatyāṃ vyāptau [sāmāṇādhikaraṇyabhāvaḥ]. tadyathā śuklaśabdaḥ svābhidheyaguṇamātraviśiṣṭadravyābhidhānāt, saty api dravye madhurādīn nākṣipati. tataś cātadbhedatvam. evam atrāpi prasaṅgaḥ.

#### English

But to those who say that a class term just signifies something as belonging to a class since then there could be coordination, establishing a connection would be feasible, and its use would be constant:

Not something belonging to a class due to non-independence.
{: .quote}

In this case, the term ‘entity’ would not signify a substance directly but merely as subordinate to the term’s form and class. So, it would not necessitate the object’s belonging to any subdivisions such as ‘pot’, etc. So, ‘pot’ would not be a subdivision of ‘entity’ and the terms could never be coordinated. After all, there can be no coordination without logical entailment. For instance, the term ‘white’ signifies a substance as qualified only by that quality of the substance which the term expresses. So it does not necessitate that further qualities such as sweetness be present in that substance, though they may in fact occur. So ‘sweet’ is not a subdivision of ‘white’. It would be a problem were the same to apply here.
