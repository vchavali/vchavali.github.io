# Diṅnāga on the Incommensurability of Experience and Thought

A translation and discussion of the Pramāṇasamuccayaḥ, chapter V, verses 1-4 and svavr̥ttiḥ. 

## Discussion

In the fifth chapter of his magnum opus, *On Measurement* (Pramāṇasamuccayaḥ), Diṅnāga develops one of his most profound and disturbing insights into the nature of human cognition: the incommensurability of experience with thought.

The incommensurability thesis (pramāṇavyavasthāvādaḥ) states that there can be no transfer of information between experiences and concepts. This means that we can never justify any claim with reference to experience nor claim to know the contents of any experiential state. It creates a symmetry between the first and third person perspective. We could no longer say to someone, “You don't know how I feel!”. Since, if Diṅnāga is right, then *we* don’t know how we feel either. And it leaves the status of empirical knowledge in an uncertain place. What does it mean to say that I know facts about the natural world if the information captured by that knowledge does not pass from nature to mind through sensation?

The incommensurability thesis separates our idea of knowledge into two distinct conceptions. On the one hand, we think of knowledge under a metaphor of *illumination*. For something to be known is for it to be present to us, to make a difference to *us* in our cognitive lives. The concept of luminosity is theorized as a distinctively epistemic concept in the first chapter of Diṅnāga’s work. A second conception of knowledge is as something that guides action and brings it under a rational description. We reason from diagnosis to intervention in a step-by-step process which is transparent, public, and capable of evaluation. 

The first kind of knowledge is what Diṅnāga calls “perception”. The second is what he calls “inference”. These two ideas represent two fundamentally different senses of the word “to know” and are only brought together by the illusion of common content. It is on the basis of this illusion that we give a single word to these two radically different phenomena.

The word Diṅnāga gives, which he inherits from a much older tradition, is “measure” (pramāṇam). A measure is something that, in some vague pretheoretic sense of the word, captures information about some aspect of the world: the measurable object (prameyaḥ). The result of the operation of a measure on an object is *measurement* (pramitiḥ). Traditions and philosophers prior to Diṅnāga debated the number, nature, and interrelations of the measures extensively. But the idea that the concept is split into two incommensurable domains is a hallmark of Diṅnāgian thought.

The key to understanding the incommensurability thesis lies in the nature of language. The incommensurability argument appears in the beginning of the fifth chapter of *On Measurement*, in which Diṅnāga works out his general theory of language. The Sanskrit original is lost, but it is preserved in two Tibetan translations and as excerpts quoted in Jinendrabuddhi’s Sanskrit commentary on the text. Quotations from it also survive scattered across sources throughout Sanskrit philosophical literature. The Sanskrit text reproduced in this essay is reconstructed from the extant sources by Ole Pind (2015). The translation, though, is my own.

According to this theory, the linguistic measure—the use of language to capture information about the world—is not distinct from the inferential measure. From the perspective of the theory of measures, language is just inference (na pramāṇāntaram śābdam anumānāt [1]). On this account, linguistic expressions and their mental analogues in thought convey information not by representing the properties of objects but by placing constraints on further language use.

We might compare this to an information-theoretic account of information content where the realization of some random variable conveys information about a system by constraining the possible micro-states that the system could exist in. In the same way, the acquisition of linguistic information about some object constrains the further possibilities of what could be said about that object. 

Linguistic information on the final analysis ends up as nothing more than a symbol’s form (sounds, shapes, or mental appearances) equipped with what Diṅnāga calls “exclusion” (apoha) relations to other information, encoding how symbols block each other. Meaning, in this view, is not a relation between language and the world but a relation internal to language itself. And these relations end up just being relations of entailment and negation. So, semantics is nothing other than logic. This comes to be known as the theory of exclusion (apohavādaḥ).

Before detailing this account, however, Diṅnāga offers an argument for the incommensurability thesis as a motivation for developing his inferential theory of meaning. In what remains of this introduction (prior to the translation itself), I want to give a brief overview and reconstruction of this argument.

Diṅnāga begins by restating the claim just discussed, that there are only two fundamental measures: perception and inference. Prior authors including other Buddhists (such as Vasubandhu) and non-buddhist philosophers (such as Vātsyāyana, author of the Nyāyasūtrabhāṣyam) generally accepted linguistic expressions as constituting a third kind of measure. On these older views, a perceptual experience captures some information about an object by rendering its properties in sensory terms. On one common-sense account entertained by Dharmakīrti (a major post-Diṅnāgian figure), sensory features represent properties of objects through a pattern of correlation such that the same properties would trigger the same sensory features across different perceptions. A linguistic expression could capture the same information by representing the object’s properties with symbols such that the presence of the same symbol across different episodes of language use would correspond to the presence of the same properties across different speech contexts.

In this framework, symbols are “connected” (sambandhaḥ) to objects such that a given symbol may only apply to an object to which it is connected. This connection must satisfy two constraints:

1. The connection is formed on the basis of some general *use condition* (pravr̥ttinimittam) that must be satisfied by all and only connected objects.
2. Speakers of the language must learn this condition in order to use the symbol.

What is communicated by the use of a symbol is just that its object satisfies its use condition. 

But Diṅnāga argues that no perceptible features can serve as the use condition for the symbols applied to percieved objects. 

The argument turns on the analysis of a family of symbols called *class terms* (jātiśabdāḥ) which are characterized by their being subject to *subdivision* and *coordination* (bhedārthair apṛthakśruteḥ [2cd]). The idea is that class terms can represent the same fact at different levels of granularity (subdivision), such as calling a shade of color “blue” vs “teal”. In this case, the two terms can be used used together (coordination) in the same speech context such that they are in some sense conditioned by the same thing in that context but are not synonymous across contexts. The sense in which two different class terms can be conditioned by the same fact is itself something to be theorized. But, the pre-theoretical data in need of theorizing are sentences such as:

1. This color is blue.
2. This vehicle is a car.
3. This entity is a substance.

etc.

The point is most clearly seen (no pun intended) in the first case. The words “color” and “blue” co-occur in a sentence in virtue of one and the same perceptual feature. It is because that feature is a color that the word “color” can be used in the sentence describing it. And it is because it is blue that the word “blue” can occur in the same sentence.

The question for us then is this. What structure must the use conditions for two coordinated class terms have given that:

1. Coordinated terms are not synonymous
2. The same feature satisfies the respective use conditions of both terms

Whatever theory of class terms we hold must satisfy the two constraints on term coordination along with the earlier two constraints on semantic connections. These four constraints must hold for *any* theory of class terms—and therefore any theory about how language captures general information about facts.

Now consider the following account for the use condition of a class term. Suppose that any of the various subdivisions of a class term like ‘blue’—‘teal’, ‘turquoise’, ‘navy’, etc.—can act as a separate condition for the parent term. So, something is ‘blue’ just in the case that it is ‘teal’ *or* ‘turquoise’ *or* ‘navy’, etc. Diṅnāga refers to this as a class term directly signifying its subdivisions (jātiśabdo bhedānāṃ [2a]).

However, a class term cannot directly signify its subdivisions because the use condition for a class term does not, in principle, fix the number of subdivisions it can have. Nothing about the meaning of the word ‘blue’ precludes the possibility of encountering a shade of blue you have never seen before, no matter how many blue shades you have encountered thus far. This means that the use condition for a class term that directly signifies its subdivisions would not have a fixed number of elements. But such a condition can never be learned. And this would violate the second constraint on semantic connections. Diṅnāga refers to this as the problem of infinity (ānantyāt. [2b1]).

On the other hand, if we allow class terms to have different use conditions across different applications, then we would not need to learn the full disjunction to apply a class term in coordination with any particular subdivision. But this would violate the first constraint on semantic connections. Since one class term could operate under multiple use conditions, we would not know which condition is operational in a particular use and would remain in doubt about what information the term is meant to convey. Diṅnāga calls this the problem of inconstancy (vyabhicārataḥ. [2b2]).

Another possibility is that the use condition for a class term involves a completely different object feature than the terms for its subdivision. That is to say, ‘blue’ and ‘teal’ simply refer to different perceptual features. But then, obviously, they would violate the second constraint on term coordination. Or they refer to the same feature and end up synonymous, violating the first constraint on term coordination. Diṅnāga refers to this as the problem of coordination (bhedārthair apṛthakśruteḥ. [2cd]). 

There is, however, another way to understand this possibility. Namely, that each class term involves a different condition but one and the same object feature satisfies both conditions. Diṅnāga refers to this idea as a class term signifying an object as having a type (tadvato [4a]). Where two different class terms are coordinated, their object has two different types. In this case, the shared object between two class terms becomes the basis for their coordination. And the difference between the types forms the basis for the difference in meaning. Thus the problem of coordination is easily answered.

In response to this, Diṅnāga provides a deeper reason for why the use conditions for class terms in a class-subdivision relation cannot involve distinct types. This is because then their semantic content would be logically independent—the class term condition would not imply the possibility of subdivision and the subdivision term condition would not entail the class term condition. So, their connection to the same features would be an empirical fact about their respective types, but not a logical fact about their use conditions.

But, this is not how the relationship between class and subdivision works. There is no such thing as a generic blue shade. We can only conceive of particular shades, never a generic ‘blue’. Similarly, when we learn that a shade of color is ‘teal’, we automatically understand that it is ‘blue’, assuming we also know the term ‘blue’. The conditions of these terms are logically connected—semantically connected—such that one cannot be fully separated from the other. We cannot treat them as independent conditions that merely *happen* to be satisfied by the same features. Diṅnāga calls this the problem of non-independence (nāsvatantratvāt [4a]).

Diṅnāga’s discussion of the coordination problem ends here. However, there is another possibility worth considering. Namely, what if the use condition of a class term is complex—involving the conjunction of multiple primitive conditions. Internal relations between class and subdivision terms can be then be explained by their use conditions sharing some types. And different subdivisions would differ from each other by having some types be different between their use conditions. 

Diṅnāga does not discuss this idea explicitly. However it is not essentially different from the previous idea. We must as if by complex we mean we conceive of the class term’s use condition as consisting of a conjunction of primitives, i.e. we think of in terms of its definition. In such a case, we can simply analyze the primitive conditions—understanding that any constraints on the use conditions of the primitives will by extension impact the conditions they constitute. Since, for the complex condition to be general (infinitely subdivisible), at least some of the primitives must be general as well—the whole argument from subdivision and coordination reapply to each constituent. 

In other words, on this account, we can simply eliminate all complex class terms from the language by replacing them with their definitions everywhere they occur. In such a case, the only remaining class terms in the language are primitives. If, however, every primitive class term only applies to a finite number a subdivisions, then no logical complex involving them can apply to infinite subdivisions. Since, it is not possible to construct an infinite set through a finite number of union, intersection, and difference operations on finite sets. But if some of the primitive class terms are infinitely subdivisible, then the original triad of infinity, inconstancy, and non-independence recurs.

On the other hand, if the use conditions are not conceived as complex then their relations to their sub-conditions is not a logical feature of the complex condition. It is not something derivable from its structure. In this case, the relationship between the complex and primitive conditions is still empirical and the non-independence argument remains in force.

## Text and Translation

### The Inferential Theory of Meaning

#### Sanskrit

uktaṃ pramāṇadvayam. kecic chābdam api [pramāṇāntaraṃ manyante].

na pramāṇāntaraṃ śābdam anumānāt. tathā hi saḥ \\\\ kṛtakatvādivat svārtham anyāpohena bhāṣate [1]
{: .quote}

śabdo hi yatra viṣaye prayujyate tasya yenāṃśenāvinābhāvitvasambandhaḥ, taṃ kṛtakatvādivad arthāntaravyavacchedena dyotayati, [tasmād anumānān] na bhidyate.

#### English

We have claimed that there are just two measures. But some think that the linguistic measure is distinct:

The linguistic measure is not distinct from inference. Just as with a reason such as “because it is produced”, an expression presents its own information by excluding other information.
{: .quote}

When a linguistic expression is applied to an object it indicates that aspect of the object with which it is invariably connected by excluding other information in the same way as a reason in an inference, as in “[it is impermanent] because it is produced”. So the linguistic measure is not distinct from inference.

### Infinity and Inconstancy

#### Sanskrit

ye tv āhuḥ: jātiśabdaḥ svabhedān sarvān evāha, ukteṣu tu niyamārthaṃ viśeṣaśrutir [iti]. [tatrocyate]

na jātiśabdo bhedānāṃ [2a]
{: .quote}

“vācaka” iti vakṣyate. jātiśabdas tāvat sadādir [dravyādīnāṃ na vācakaḥ],

ānantyāt. [2b1]
{: .quote}

ānantye hi bhedānām aśakyaḥ sambandhaḥ kartum. na cākṛtasambandhe śabde ʾrthābhidhānaṃ yuktaṃ svarūpamātrapratīteḥ.

kiṃ ca,

vyabhicārataḥ. [2b2]
{: .quote}

yathā hi sacchabdo dravye vartate tathā guṇādiṣv apīti vyabhicārāt saṃśayaḥ syāt, nābhidhānam.

#### English

To those who say a class term signifies all of its subdivisions and a specific term serves only to select among them: we reply,

A class term is not of its subdivisions
{: .quote}

The word “signifier” must be supplied. A class term like ‘entity’ does not signify its subdivisions like substance etc. 

because of infinity.
{: .quote}

Subdivisions are infinite so no connection could be established. Without establishing a connection, an expression cannot convey any information since it would generate awareness only of its own form.

Moreover,

Due to inconstancy.
{: .quote}

The term ‘entity’ is applied to qualities, etc., just as it is to substances. Therefore its use is not constant and there would be doubt about its meaning. No signification would occur.

### Coordination

#### Sanskrit

yo ʾpi manyate: [jātiśabdo jātimātre tadyogamātre vā] sambandhasaukaryād avyabhicārāc ceti. tad ayuktam, [na hi tayor api]

vācako yogajātyor vā bhedārthair apṛthakśruteḥ. [2cd]
{: .quote}

tathā hi [sad dravyam, san guṇaḥ, sat karmeti bhedārthair dravyādiśabdaiḥ] sāmānādhikaraṇyaṃ na syāt. tac ca dṛṣṭam. na hi sattā [tadyogo vā] dravyaṃ guṇo vā bhavati, kiṃ tarhi, dravyasya guṇasya vā. āha ca:

vibhaktibhedo niyamād guṇaguṇyabhidhāyinoḥ \\\\ sāmānādhikaraṇyasya prasiddhir dravyaśabdayoḥ. [VP III 14.8]
{: .quote}

sambandhaś cātra sambandhidharmeṇa vācya ucyate: \\\\ tathā hi bhāvaḥ kṛtvoktaḥ, bhāvaś cānyena yujyate. [3]
{: .quote}

sambandhanaṃ hi sambandhaḥ. [so ʾnyena yujyate rāgādivat]. tasmāt sambandhidharmeṇa sambandho vācya iti kṛtvāśaṅkitaṃ svadharmeṇa tu nāsti sambandhasya vācakaḥ śabda ity idaṃ tat prati nāsti. ato naivasya jātiśabdena vācyatvam upapadyate.

#### English

Now, to those who think that a class term just signifies a type or instantiation relation since establishing a connection would then be feasible and its use would be constant: this does not work because even in this case

It does not signify a relation or a type because then there would be no coordination with terms for subdivisions.
{: .quote}

In this case, there would be no coordination of class terms with terms for subdivisions as in “a substance entity”, “a quality entity”, “an action entity”, etc. But this is observed. Moreover, neither the existence type nor its instantiation relation *is* a substance or quality. Rather, it is *of* the substance or quality. For, as [Bhartr̥hari] notes:

A property and its substrate are signified with a difference in case-ending. Coordination is only between two substrate level terms.
{: .quote}
(Vākyapadīyam III.14.8)
{: .examplecaption}

Moreover, a relation is signified with reference to a property of the relatum. In such a case, it is first treated as an action and then signified. But an action still requires a separate relation to connect it to its arguments.
{: .quote}

A relation is construed as an act of relating and connected to its relatum by a separate relation just like a quality (e.g. color). Given that a relation can only be signified with reference to a property of the relatum, the worry that an expression cannot signify a relation with respect to the relation’s own property does not arise. [But then what is signified is not the relation as such.] Therefore, a class term cannot be said to signify a relation.

### Non-independence

#### Sanskrit

[ye tv āhuḥ]\: viśeṣaśabdaiḥ [sāmānādhikaraṇyāt sambandhasaukāryād avyabhicārāc ca jātimanmātraṃ vivakṣitam iti. tatra]

tadvato nāsvatantratvāt. [4a]
{: .quote}

evam api hi sacchabdo jātisvarūpamātropasarjanaṃ dravyam āha, na sākṣād iti tadgataghaṭādibhedānākṣepād atadbhedatve sāmānādhikaraṇyābhāvaḥ. na hy asatyāṃ vyāptau [sāmāṇādhikaraṇyabhāvaḥ]. tadyathā śuklaśabdaḥ svābhidheyaguṇamātraviśiṣṭadravyābhidhānāt, saty api dravye madhurādīn nākṣipati. tataś cātadbhedatvam. evam atrāpi prasaṅgaḥ.

#### English

But to those who say that a class term just signifies something as having a type since then there could be coordination, establishing a connection would be feasible, and its use would be constant:

Not something as having a type due to non-independence.
{: .quote}

In this case, the term ‘entity’ would not signify a substance directly but merely as subordinate to the term’s form and type. So, it would not necessitate the object’s belonging to any subdivisions such as ‘pot’, etc. So, ‘pot’ would not be a subdivision of ‘entity’ and the terms could never be coordinated. After all, there can be no coordination without logical entailment. For instance, the term ‘white’ signifies a substance as qualified only by that quality of the substance which the term expresses. So it does not necessitate that further qualities such as sweetness be present in that substance, though they may in fact occur. So ‘sweet’ is not a subdivision of ‘white’. It would be a problem were the same to apply here.
