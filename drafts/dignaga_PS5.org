
* Introduction

What follows is one of my favorite arguments in the history of philosophy—and arguably one of the most important. In about four verses, interspersed with brief prose comments, Diṇnāga demonstrates out one of his most profound and disturbing insights into the nature of human cognition: the incommensurability of perception and language.

The incommensurability theses (pramāṇavyavasthāvādaḥ) states that there can be no transfer of information or justification between perception and language. In the direction of perception to language, this means that we can never justify any claim with reference to experience nor claim to know the contents of any experiential state. This creates a symmetry between the first and third person perspective. It is almost a cliche to say to another, “You could never know how I feel!”. But if Diṇnāga is right then /I/ don’t know how I feel either. In the direction of language to perception, this means we can never communicate the content of our experience nor capture the structure of experience in language. Insofar as all science—all theory—is fundementally linguistic, this means there can never be a /science/ of consciousness. No theory of exprience. 

It separates our concept of knowledge into two distinct ideas. On the one hand we think of knowledge under a metaphor of /illumination/. For something to be known is for it to be present to us, to make a difference to /us/ in our cognitive lives. The concept of luminosity is theorized as a distinctively epistemic concept in the first chapter of Diṇnāga’s great work: /On Measurement/ (/Pramāṇsamuccayaḥ/). Another conception of knowledge is as something that guides action and brings it under a rational description. We reason from diagnosis to intervention in a step-by-step process which is transparent, public, and capable of evaluation. The first kind of knowledge is what Diṇnāga calls “perception”. The second is what he calls “inference”. These two ideas represent fundementally different senses of the word “to know” and are only brought together by the illusion of common content. It is on the basis of this illusion, that we give a single word to these two radically different phenomena.

The word Diṅnāga gives, which he inherits from a much older tradition, is “measure” (pramāṇam). A measure is something that (in some vague, pretheoretic sense of the word) captures information about some aspect of the world: the measurable object (prameyaḥ). The result of the operation of a measure on an object is /measurement/ (pramitiḥ). Traditions and philosophers prior to Diṇnāga debated the number, nature, and relationship of the measures extensively. But the idea that the concept is split into two incommensurable domains is a hallmark of Diṇnāgian thought.

The key to understanding the incommensurability thesis lies in the nature of language. The incommensurability argument appears in the begining of the fifth chapter of /On Measurement/, in which Diṇnāga works out his general theory of language. The sanskrit original is lost, but it is preserved in two Tibetan translations and as excerpts quoted in Jinnendrabuddhi’s sanskrit commentary on the text. Quotations from it also survive scattered across sources throughout sanskrit philosophical literature. The below quoted sanskrit text is reconstructed from the Tibetan by Ole Pin (2015). I reproduce the sanskrit and offer a new translation below.

According to this theory, the linguistic measure—i.e. the use of language to capure information about the world—is not distinct from the inferential measure. Langauge is just inference (na pramāṇāntaram śābdam anumanāt). On this account, linguistic expressions (and their mental analogues in thought) capture information not by representing the properties of objects but by placing constraints on further language use. Linguistic expressions communicate their own content by excluding the content of other expressions. As a consequence asserting one thing prohibits us from asserting other things. We might compare this to an information-theoretic account of information content where the realization of some random variable conveys information about a system by contraining the possible microstates that it can exist in.

Linguistic “content” on the final analysis ends up as nothing more that a symbols “form” (sounds, shapes, or mental appearances) equipped with exclusion relations to other symbols. meaning, on this account, is not a relation between language and the world but a relation internal to language itself. and these relations end up just being relations of entailment and contradiction. so, semantic content is nothing other than logical content and language is nothing other than inference. this comes to be known as the exclusion theory of meaning (apohavādaḥ).

Before detailing this theory, however, Diṇnāga offers an argument for the incommensurability thesis as the motivation for developing the inferential theory of meaning just outlined. In what remains of this introduction (prior to the translation itself), I want to give a brief overview and reconstruction of this argument.

Diṇnāga begins by restating the claim just discussed, that there are only two fundemental measures: perception and inference. Prior authors including other Buddhists (such as Vasubandhu) and non-buddhist philosophers such as Vātsyāyana (the author of the Nyāyasūtrabhāṣya) generally accepted linguistic expressions as constituting a third distinct kind of measure. On these older views, a perceptual experience captured some information about an object by rendering its properties in sensory terms. On one common-sense view entertained by Dharmakīrti (a major post-Diṇnāgian figure), sensory features represented properties of objects through a pattern of correlation such that the same properties would trigger the same sensory features across different perceptions. A linguistic expression could capture the same information by representing the object’s properties with symbols such that the presence of the same symbol across different episodes of language use would correspond to the presence of the same properties across speech contexts.

Under such a framework view, symbols are “connected” (sambandhaḥ) to objects such that a given symbol may only apply to an object to which it is connected. These connections must satisfy two contraints:

1. This connection is formed on the basis of some general /use condition/ that must be satisfied by all and only connected objects.
2. Speakers of the language must learn this condition in order to use the symbol.

What is communicated, on this framework, by the use of a symbol is just that its object satisfies the use condition. Diṇnāga argues that actually no facts about objects can serve as the use condition for the symbols applied to them. And, in particular, no /perceptual feature/ can ever act as the use condition for a symbol.

The argument turns on the relationship between a family of symbols called /class terms/, which are to be distinguished primarily from proper names by being subject to /subdivision/ (bhedārthair apṛthakśruteḥ [2cd]). The key idea is that class terms can represent the same fact at different levels of granularity, such as calling a shade of color “blue” vs “teal”. In this case, the two terms can be used in coordination with each other such that they are in “some sense” conditioned by the same thing in a given context but are not synonymous across contexts. The “sense” in which they are co-conditioned is itself something to be theorized. But, the pre-theoretical data in need of theorizing are sentences such as:

1. This color is blue.
2. This vehicle is a car.
3. This entity is a substance.
etc.

The point is most clearly seen (no pun intended) in the first case. The words “color” and “blue” co-occur in sentences in virtue of one and the same perceptual feature. It is because that feature is a color that the word “color” can be used in the sentence describing it. And it is because it is blue that the word “blue” can occur in the same sentence.

The question for us then is: what is the relationship between the conditions for the words “color” and “blue” such that:

1. The words are not synonymous
2. The same feature satisfies the condition for both words

Whatever theory of class terms we hold must satisfy the two constraints on term coordination along with the earlier three constraints on use conditions. These five constraints must hold for /any/ theory of class terms—and therefore any theory about how language captures information about facts.

Now consider the following account for the condition of a class term. Suppose that any of the various subdivisions of a class term like ‘blue’—e.g. ‘teal’, ‘turqouis’, ‘navy’, etc.—can act as a separate condition for the parent term. So, something is ‘blue’ just in the case that it is ‘teal’ /or/ ‘turqouis’ /or/ ‘navy’, etc. Diṇnāga refers to this as a class term directly signifying its subdivisions (jātiśabdo bhedānāṃ [2a]).

Diṇnāge begins the reduction by showing that a class term cannot directly signify its subdivisions. The reason is that the use condition for a class term does not, in general, fix the number of subdivisions it can have. Nothing about the meaning of the word ‘blue’ precludes the possibility of encountering a shade of blue you have never seen before—no matter how many blue shades you have encountered thus far. This means that the condition for a class term that directly signifies its subdivisions would have an infinite number of elements. But such a condition can never be learned. And this would violate the second constraint on conditions. Diṇnāga refers to this as the problem of infinity (ānantyāt. [2b1]).

If, on the other hand, we did not need to learn the full set of disjunctives to use the term then it would only to serve to inform us of the satisfaction of just the part of the set of conditions we have actually learned. Then, when applied to objects out of this set it would not apply on the basis of the connection which has been formed for it, violating the first constraint. In effect, it would be operating under a different condition in each domain of its use and would fail to give definite information regarding which domain it is operating under—resulting only in doubt regarding the information it is meant to convey. Diṇnāga calls this the problem of inconstancy (vyabhicārataḥ. [2b2]).

Another possibility is that the condition for a class term involves a completely different object feature than the terms for its subdivision. That is to say, ‘blue’ and ‘teal’ simply refer to different perceptual features. But then, obviously, they would violate the second constraint on term coordination. Or they refer to the same feature and end up synonymous, violating the first constraint. Diṇnāga refers to this as the problem of coordination (bhedārthair apṛthakśruteḥ. [2cd]). 

There is, however, another way to understand this possibility. Namely, that each class term involves a different condition but one and the same object feature satisfies both conditions. Under such a proposal the problem of coordination becomes a psuedoproblem. In response to this, Diṇnāga provides a deeper reason for why class terms in a class-subdivision relation cannot have different conditions. This is because their semantic content would be logically independent—the class term condition would not imply the possibility of subdivision and the subdivision term condition would not entail the class term condition. Their co-satisfaction by the same features would be an empirical fact about them, but not a logical fact.

But, this is not how the relation between class and subdivision works. There is no such this as a generic blue shade. We can only concieve of particular shades, never a generic ‘blue’. Similarly, when we learn that a shade of color is ‘teal’, we automatically understand that it is ‘blue’, assuming we also have know the term ‘blue’. The conditions of these terms are logically connected—semantically connected—such that one cannot be fully seperated from the other and each treated as independent conditions that merely /happen/ to be satisfied by the same features. Diṇnāga calls this the problem on non-independence (verse 4a).

Diṇnāga’s discussion of the coordination problem ends here. However, there is another possibility worth considering. Namely, what if the condition of a class term is complex—involving the conjuction of multiple other more primitive conditions. In such a case, the internal relations between class and subdivision terms can be explained in terms of a shared kernel, with different subdivisions differing from each other via additional conditions in the conjunction. Diṇnāga does not discuss this issue explicitly perhaps because he takes it for granted that all complex conditions bottom out into simple conditions that are still subdivisible. This assumption is not obviously true, though it does seem like it would be acceptible to his main interlocutors given the account of universals defended by, e.g., the Naiyyāyikas. However, even if we do not accept the assumption for class terms in general, something more can be said in this regard where specifically perceptual features are concerned.

The key idea is to borrow a proposal from Dharmakīrti, called the co-experience constraint (/sahopalambaniyamaḥ/). The co-experience constraint is meant to be a criterion of identity for perceptual features. Dharmakīrti uses this in a different context but we can apply it here. The criterion is simple. It states:

(Co-experience Constraint) If it is not possible to isolate two perceptual features from each other in any experience, then they are one and the same feature.

In other words, what it /means/ for a perceptual experience to have distinct features is for elements of that experience to be separatable in our attention. Otherwise, we have no criterion of identification for them as distinct features in the first place. What is it that makes, e.g., smell and taste distinct features? Or color and texture? etc. It is that we can isolate them, attend to them, focus on them separately. Otherwise, nothing distinguishes one feature from another. Whereas for “blue” and “teal”, there is nothing about the visual feature that can be isolated in the experience of that color and connected with one term or another. Notions like hue, contrast, and shade are also only separable as abstactions but not isolatable as distinct feature in an experience. This is another way of saying that certain sensory experiences are /simple/.

If we accept this criterion, then perceptual features, by definition, cannot involve complex conditions. Otherwise, their conditions would not feature as the contents of direct sensory acquiantance. And if we cannot have sensory acquaintaince with conditions we have no way of knowing if a given perceptual features satisfies the condition of a given symbol.

There is one other possibility to consider in this respect—namely making a metaphysical move in response to Dharmakīrti’s phenomenological (experiential) criterion of identification for perceptual features. This is the approach suggested by Vaiśeṣika authors like Praśastapāda, who argue for the occurance of what he calls epistemic inseparability (ayutasiddhiḥ) relations between certain kinds of objects. The idea, especially as developed by the late-classical Naiyyāyika, Jayanta Bhaṭṭa, is that certain objects—prototypically sensory qualities and the substances they inhere in—enjoy an especially close sort of relationship such that they do not register as distinct items in any measurement (any sensory or inferential grasping) where they occur together. They are only separable by observing multiple instances and abstracting from them. So, the redness of the apple is not separable from the substance of the apple in any single instance. But, comparing apples of different colors, we can separate the colors out conceptually through contrast.

The philosopher Kumārila Bhaṭṭa (normally a critique of Diṇnāga’s project) levels a famous attack against such a relation of  epistemic inseparability. The argument starts with the observation that all relations are in some sense posterior to their relata. That is to say, they depend on their relata for their existence and informational content. Relations are themselves empty of content—and so unmeasurable—when abstracted from all relata. However, if relata are in a relation of epistemic inseparability then they are informationally incomplete without their relation to each other. This means that the relation depends for its content on the relata—and the relata depend for their content on the relation. This is circular, which Kumārila takes to be decisive against the account. There are unresolved issues here regarding what exactly constitutes the informational incompleteness of the relata and to what extent this poses a genuine metaphysical problem rather than an epistemic one. But, we will rest the case here and introduce the text itself.

* Original Text

** 1
uktaṃ pramāṇadvayam. kecic chābdam api <pramāṇāntaraṃ manyante>.

na pramāṇāntaraṃ śābdam anumānāt. tathā hi saḥ \\ kṛtakatvādivat svārtham anyāpohena bhāṣate [1]

śabdo hi yatra viṣaye prayujyate tasya yenāṃśenāvinābhāvitvasambandhaḥ, taṃ kṛtakatvādivad arthāntaravyavacchedena dyotayati, <tasmād anumānān> na bhidyate.

*** Translation

We have claimed that there are just two measures but some think that the linguistic measure is distinct:

^The linguistic measure is not distinct from inference. Just as with a reason such as “because it is produced”, an expression presents its own content through the exclusion of other content.

When a linguistic expression is applied to an object it signifies that aspect of the object with which it is invariably connected through the exclusion of other content in the same way as a reason like “because it is produced” in an inference. So the two are not distinct measures.

** 2

ye tv āhuḥ: jātiśabdaḥ svabhedān sarvān evāha, ukteṣu tu niyamārthaṃ viśeṣaśrutir <iti>. <tatrocyate>

na jātiśabdo bhedānāṃ [2a]

“vācaka” iti vakṣyate. jātiśabdas tāvat sadādir <dravyādīnāṃ na vācakaḥ>,

ānantyāt. [2b1]

ānantye hi bhedānām aśakyaḥ sambandhaḥ kartum. na cākṛtasambandhe śabde ʾrthābhidhānaṃ yuktaṃ svarūpamātrapratīteḥ.

kiṃ ca,

vyabhicārataḥ. [2b2]


yathā hi sacchabdo dravye vartate tathā guṇādiṣv apīti vyabhicārāt saṃśayaḥ syāt, nābhidhānam.

*** Translation

To those who say a class term signifies all of its subdivisions and a specific term serves only to select among them—we reply:

^A class term is not of its subdivisions

The word “signifier” must be supplied. A class term like ‘entity’ does not signify its subdivisions like substance etc. —

^ because of infinity.

Subdivisions are infinite so no connection to them can be established. Without establishing a connection, an expression cannot signify any content since it would generate awareness only of its own form.

Moreover —

^Due to inconstancy.

The term ‘entity’ is applied to qualities, etc., just as it is to substances. Therefore its use is not constant and there would be doubt about its content. No signification would occur.

** 3

yo ʾpi manyate: <jātiśabdo jātimātre tadyogamātre vā> sambandhasaukaryād avyabhicārāc ceti. tad ayuktam, <na hi tayor api>

vācako yogajātyor vā bhedārthair apṛthakśruteḥ. [2cd]

tathā hi <sad dravyam, san guṇaḥ, sat karmeti bhedārthair dravyādiśabdaiḥ> sāmānādhikaraṇyaṃ na syāt. tac ca dṛṣṭam. na hi sattā <tadyogo vā > dravyaṃ guṇo vā bhavati, kiṃ tarhi, dravyasya guṇasya vā. āha ca:

vibhaktibhedo niyamād guṇaguṇyabhidhāyinoḥ \\ sāmānādhikaraṇyasya prasiddhir dravyaśabdayoḥ. [VP III 14.8]

sambandhaś cātra sambandhidharmeṇa vācya ucyate: \\ tathā hi bhāvaḥ kṛtvoktaḥ, bhāvaś cānyena yujyate. [3]

sambandhanaṃ hi sambandhaḥ. <so ʾnyena yujyate rāgādivat>. tasmāt sambandhidharmeṇa sambandho vācya iti kṛtvāśaṅkitaṃ svadharmeṇa tu nāsti sambandhasya vācakaḥ śabda ity idaṃ tat prati nāsti. ato naivasya jātiśabdena vācyatvam upapadyate.

*** Translation

Now, to those who think that a class term just signifies a class or a class’s relation to its instances since a connection between expression and content would then be feasible and the term’s use would be constant: this does not work because even in this case —

^ It does not signify a relation or a class because then there would be no coordination with terms for subdivisions.

In this case, there would be no coordination of class terms with terms for subdivisions as in “a substance entity”, “a quality entity”, “an action entity”, etc. But this is observed. Moreover, neither the existence class nor its relation to its instances /is/ a substance or quality. Rather, it is /of/ the substance or quality. For, as [Bhartr̥hari] notes:

^^A property and its substrate are signified with a difference in case-ending. Coordination is only between two substrate level terms. [VP ĪI 14.8]

^Moreover, a relation is signified with reference to a property of the relatum. In such a case, it is first treated as an action and then signified. But an action still requires a seperate relation to connect it to its arguments.

A relation is construed as an act of relating and connected to its relatum by a separate relation just like a quality (e.g. color). Given that a relation can only be signified with reference to a property of the relatum, the worry that an expression cannot signify a relation with respect to the relation’s own property does not arise. [But then what is signified is not the relation as such.] Therefore, a class term cannot be said to signify a relation.

** 4

<ye tv āhuḥ>: viśeṣaśabdaiḥ <sāmānādhikaraṇyāt sambandhasaukāryād avyabhicārāc ca jātimanmātraṃ vivakṣitam iti. tatra>

tadvato nāsvatantratvāt. [4a]

evam api hi sacchabdo jātisvarūpamātropasarjanaṃ dravyam āha, na sākṣād iti tadgataghaṭādibhedānākṣepād atadbhedatve sāmānādhikaraṇyābhāvaḥ. na hy asatyāṃ vyāptau <sāmāṇādhikaraṇyabhāvaḥ>. tadyathā śuklaśabdaḥ svābhidheyaguṇamātraviśiṣṭadravyābhidhānāt, saty api dravye madhurādīn nākṣipati. tataś cātadbhedatvam. evam atrāpi prasaṅgaḥ.

*** Translation

But to those who say that a class term just signifies something as belonging to a class since there could then be coordination with terms for subdivisions, a connection between expression and content would be feasible, and the term’s use would be constant:

^Not something belonging to class T due to non-independence.

In this a case, the term ‘entity’ would not signify a substance directly but merely as subordinate to the expression of some class. So, it would fail to implicate any subdivisions such as ‘pot’, etc., pertaining to that object. But then ‘pot’ would [be an independent class] and not a subdivision of ‘entity’. So the terms could never be coordinated since there can be no coordination between two terms expressing logically independent content. For instance, the term ‘white’ signifies a substance as qualified only by that quality of the substance which the term expresses. So it does not implicate further qualities such as sweetness though they may be present in that substance. So ‘sweet’ is not a subdivision of ‘white’. It would be a problem were the same to apply here.
